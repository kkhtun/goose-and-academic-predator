    1. How can I recognise a predatory journal, publisher or conference? 
A predatory journal, publisher, or conference can be recognized through several warning signs related to transparency, peer review, editorial quality, and ethical publishing practices. Common indicators include promises of extremely rapid publication or guaranteed acceptance, aggressive spam email invitations, unclear or hidden publication fees, fake impact factors, and false claims of indexing in reputable databases. Predatory outlets often lack rigorous peer review, provide poor-quality websites with grammatical errors, and list editorial board members whose identities or affiliations cannot be verified.
In addition, deceptive conferences may advertise broad or unrelated scientific topics, use prestigious-sounding names, and prioritize registration payments over academic quality. Researchers can protect themselves by independently verifying indexing claims through trusted databases such as Scopus or Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ), consulting institutional guidance, and evaluating whether the journal or conference follows recognized ethical and scholarly standards.
    2. What are the criteria for evaluating a scientific journal? 
The evaluation of a scientific journal is based on several criteria related to academic quality, transparency, and scientific credibility. Important factors include the presence of a rigorous peer-review process, a qualified and verifiable editorial board, clear ethical and publication policies, and indexing in recognized databases such as Scopus or Web of Science. Researchers should also examine the quality of published articles, the journal’s scope, citation metrics, archiving systems, and the transparency of publication fees.
Additionally, a reputable journal demonstrates consistency in editorial standards, provides accurate contact and publisher information, and follows established guidelines for research integrity and plagiarism prevention. Evaluating these criteria helps researchers distinguish legitimate journals from predatory or low-quality publishing outlets, thereby supporting informed academic publishing decisions.
    3. Are You Submitting Your Research to a Trusted Journal?
Submitting research to a trusted journal is essential for ensuring scientific credibility, professional recognition, and long-term academic impact. Researchers should also verify the qualifications of the editorial board, the quality of previously published articles, and the transparency of publication fees and review procedures.
Carefully evaluating a journal before submission helps protect researchers from predatory publishing practices that may damage academic reputation and reduce the visibility and credibility of their work. Choosing a reliable journal therefore supports research integrity, increases citation potential, and contributes positively to long-term scholarly and professional development.
    4. Does the journal have a well-established reputation in your field? Does the journal clearly describe its peer review process?

These scholars usually have strong publication records and are actively involved in their fields of specialization, which helps ensure the quality, credibility, and integrity of the journal’s editorial and peer-review processes.

    6. Are the publication fees transparent and reasonable?
Legitimate journals clearly disclose publication fees, including Article Processing Charges (APCs), submission costs, and any additional publication expenses before the submission process begins. Transparent and reasonable fees demonstrate ethical publishing practices and allow researchers to make informed decisions without unexpected financial obligations.
In contrast, predatory journals often hide fees until after acceptance, apply excessive or unclear charges, or pressure authors into rapid payment. Evaluating whether publication costs are openly explained, justified by editorial services, and comparable to standards within the field is therefore an important criterion for assessing a journal’s credibility and integrity.
    7. How does science describe predatory journals?
Science describes predatory journals as deceptive or unethical publishing outlets that exploit the academic need to publish while failing to provide legitimate editorial and peer-review services. These journals often prioritize financial profit over scientific quality and integrity, accepting manuscripts with little or no rigorous evaluation in exchange for publication fees.
Predatory journals are commonly characterized by misleading indexing claims, fake impact factors, aggressive email solicitation, nontransparent publication practices, and unqualified or fabricated editorial boards. Researchers and academic institutions view them as a threat to scholarly communication because they can spread unreliable research, weaken trust in scientific publishing, and damage the credibility of authors and academic institutions.

    16. Why is it important to have adequate peer review time, rather than too short?
Adequate peer review time is important because rigorous scientific evaluation requires careful analysis of a manuscript’s methodology, originality, accuracy, ethical standards, and overall contribution to the field. Qualified reviewers need sufficient time to examine the research critically, provide constructive feedback, and recommend necessary revisions that improve the quality and reliability of the study.
In contrast, an excessively short review process may indicate superficial or nonexistent peer review, which is common in predatory publishing practices. Rapid acceptance without detailed evaluation can allow methodological errors, plagiarism, or unreliable findings to be published, thereby weakening scientific integrity and reducing trust in the research.

    17. Journals' blacklists can never be updated: why?
Journal blacklists can never be completely updated because the academic publishing landscape changes continuously, with new journals and publishers appearing, changing names, merging, or modifying their practices over time. Predatory publishers often adapt quickly by creating new websites, launching additional journals, or rebranding to avoid detection, making it difficult for any blacklist to remain fully accurate and comprehensive.
In addition, the evaluation of journals is sometimes subjective and context-dependent, since publishing quality and editorial standards may improve or decline over time. For this reason, many researchers and institutions recommend combining blacklist information with independent evaluation methods, such as verifying indexing status, peer-review transparency, editorial quality, and ethical publishing practices.

Such practices are commonly associated with predatory journals and may indicate a lack of transparency, unethical financial practices, and prioritization of profit over academic integrity and scholarly standards.
    54. Is the fee unusually low or suspiciously negotiable?
An unusually low or suspiciously negotiable publication fee can sometimes be a warning sign of predatory publishing practices, especially when combined with weak peer review, aggressive solicitation, or poor editorial transparency. Legitimate journals generally establish stable and clearly defined fees that reflect the costs of editorial management, peer review, archiving, and publication services.
If a journal rapidly reduces fees, offers unexpected discounts without clear policies, or negotiates publication charges in an inconsistent manner, researchers should carefully evaluate the journal’s credibility and publishing standards. Although low-cost or fee-free journals can be legitimate, especially in nonprofit or institutional publishing models, publication costs should always be assessed together with peer-review quality, indexing status, and ethical transparency.
    55. Are you asked to pay via Western Union, MoneyGram, cryptocurrency, or personal accounts?
Requests to pay publication fees through Western Union, MoneyGram, cryptocurrency, or personal bank accounts can be serious warning signs of predatory or fraudulent publishing practices. Legitimate journals and publishers generally use secure, transparent, and traceable payment systems linked to official institutional or corporate accounts, ensuring accountability and financial protection for authors and institutions.
Unregulated or difficult-to-trace payment methods may indicate attempts to avoid oversight, conceal financial activities, or pressure researchers into unsafe transactions. Researchers should therefore verify the publisher’s identity, payment procedures, and institutional legitimacy before making any financial transactions related to publication or conference participation.
    56. Are invoices informal, poorly formatted, or sent from non-institutional emails?
Informal or poorly formatted invoices, especially those sent from non-institutional email addresses, can indicate weak professional standards or potentially predatory publishing practices. Legitimate publishers typically issue official invoices that include clear institutional information, payment details, tax identification, publication references, and professional formatting consistent with recognized academic and financial procedures.
Invoices sent from generic email services, containing grammatical errors, missing contact information, or unclear payment instructions may raise concerns about the authenticity and credibility of the journal or publisher. Researchers should carefully verify financial communications to avoid fraud, hidden fees, or unethical publishing operations.
    57. Does the publisher operate hundreds or thousands of journals with minimal differentiation?
A publisher that operates hundreds or thousands of journals with minimal differentiation may raise concerns about editorial quality and publishing oversight, particularly if many journals have overlapping scopes, repetitive titles, or unclear academic focus. In some cases, predatory publishers rapidly create large numbers of journals primarily to maximize publication fees rather than to support distinct scholarly communities or maintain rigorous peer-review standards.
However, operating many journals is not automatically evidence of predatory behavior, since some large legitimate publishers also manage extensive journal portfolios across multiple disciplines. Researchers should therefore evaluate additional factors such as peer-review quality, editorial transparency, indexing status, ethical standards, and the scientific quality of published articles before assessing the credibility of the publisher.
    58. Are multiple journals sharing identical aims, scopes, or templates?
Multiple journals sharing identical aims, scopes, or website templates can be a warning sign of poor editorial differentiation or potentially predatory publishing practices. Legitimate journals usually maintain clearly defined disciplinary focuses, distinct editorial identities, and specialized scholarly objectives that reflect the needs of specific academic communities.
Predatory publishers, however, often create large numbers of nearly identical journals using repetitive descriptions, duplicated website structures, and overlapping subject areas in order to attract more submissions and publication fees. Such similarities may indicate limited editorial oversight, weak academic specialization, and a stronger emphasis on commercial expansion than on genuine scientific contribution.
    59. Is the publisher known for paper mills, fabricated data, or mass-produced articles?
A publisher associated with paper mills, fabricated data, or mass-produced articles raises serious concerns about research integrity and editorial reliability. Paper mills are organizations that produce fraudulent or low-quality manuscripts for profit, often involving plagiarism, manipulated peer review, falsified data, or fabricated authorship. Publishers that fail to detect or prevent such practices may contribute to the spread of unreliable scientific information.
Researchers should carefully evaluate whether the publisher has a history of retractions, ethical investigations, or criticism from the academic community. Resources such as Retraction Watch and official publisher ethics statements can help identify concerns related to scientific misconduct and publication quality. Association with publishers linked to unethical practices may damage academic credibility and weaken trust in published research.
    60. Are retraction policies, ethics statements, or COPE membership missing or fake?
Missing or fake retraction policies, ethics statements, or claims of membership in organizations such as Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) can be strong indicators of predatory or unreliable publishing practices. Legitimate journals clearly present policies regarding plagiarism, conflicts of interest, corrections, retractions, and research misconduct in order to ensure transparency, accountability, and scientific integrity.
If these policies are absent, vague, copied from other sources, or falsely presented, researchers may question whether the journal follows recognized ethical standards or properly manages publication misconduct. Independent verification of ethical memberships and publishing policies is therefore essential for assessing the credibility and reliability of a journal or publisher.

Researchers should carefully compare the publisher’s practices with recognized standards of scholarly publishing, including transparency, ethical policies, indexing verification, and editorial accountability.     62. Is the conference labelled as pseudo, fake, or deceptive?
A conference labelled as pseudo, fake, or deceptive is generally considered unreliable because it may lack genuine scientific standards, rigorous peer review, and credible academic organization. Such conferences often prioritize registration fees over scholarly quality and may use misleading titles, false affiliations, or exaggerated claims about keynote speakers, indexing, or international recognition.
Researchers should carefully verify the conference organizers, scientific committee, peer-review procedures, publication arrangements, and institutional affiliations before participating. Conferences identified as deceptive may damage academic reputation, reduce the value of presented research, and limit opportunities for meaningful scholarly networking and collaboration.
    63. Are conferences promising publication guarantees?
Conferences that promise guaranteed publication should be approached with caution because legitimate academic conferences normally require submitted papers to undergo peer review and scientific evaluation before acceptance or publication. Publication decisions are generally based on research quality, originality, methodological rigor, and relevance to the conference scope.
Guarantees of acceptance or publication may indicate weak editorial standards or predatory conference practices that prioritize financial gain over academic integrity. Researchers should therefore verify the conference’s review procedures, organizers, publication partners, and indexing claims before submitting their work or paying registration fees.
    64. Are keynote speakers listed without confirmation or with fake profiles?
